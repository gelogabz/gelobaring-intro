01

The same concept, five different names.

Cloud marketplaces are where software companies list, price, and sell through AWS, Microsoft, Google, Snowflake, and Oracle. The rules are contractual, regional, and different on every platform.

A sales team has to answer questions like “can this customer pay from their committed cloud spend?” and “who authorizes a channel resale in EMEA?” The answer is usually spread across six documentation sites, three internal docs, and one person who happens to know. The same concept is called a CPPO on AWS, an MPO on Azure, and an MCPO on Google. Searching any of those acronyms returns marketing pages, not definitions.

One concept, five vocabularies

Channel resale offer Channel Partner Private Offer (CPPO) Multiparty Private Offer (MPO) Marketplace Channel Private Offer (MCPO)
Contract of record Agreement Entitlement Subscription
Committed spend Enterprise Discount Program (EDP) MACC Committed Use Discount

The gap was not a lack of documentation. It was the absence of one place where the vocabulary, the mechanics, and the next action lived together and stayed current.

02

How the 2026 system fits together.

I did not write a one-off handbook. I built a loop: capture what changed, decide what matters, publish it, and fold the durable parts back into a permanent reference.

Capture

13 sources across five platforms feed the reference and the What’s New archive. A separate 16-source watchlist drives three domain agents weekly, plus an event-triggered pass within 48 hours of a major announcement.

Diff & score

Content hashes are compared against stored snapshots. Each candidate gets a 1–5 score and is routed to the briefing, a reference update, or the discard pile.

Human gate

I review the reasoning before opening a draft. A rejected topic costs one read, not a rewrite. Nothing publishes on its own.

Publish

Approved items become a briefing edition or a stamped reference section, with source links and a last-verified date.

The process documents were shared work. I owned discovery and orchestration, and integrated a UX outline and writing guide produced by a designer and a content lead. The reference site, publishing site, and ingestion script were mine.

Layer 1 · Permanent

Reference

507 terms, 1,477 curated cross-links, 21 learning paths, 37 guided walkthroughs, and a cross-platform comparison map.

Layer 2 · Recurring

Briefing

Five editions. Each starts with a theme, compares the hyperscalers, and ends with a practical implication.

Layer 3 · Process

Operations

Seven agent specs, three skill documents, explicit review gates, and documented failure modes. A model for keeping the other layers current.

The curriculum sits beside the loop. It sequences the reference into a seven-week path and pairs every module with a quiz.

Working principles

  1. Start with the decision. Write down what the reader needs to do differently before choosing a format or a tool.
  2. Ship something reviewable. Deliver in phases a collaborator can inspect, not one big reveal at the end.
  3. Record the trade-off. Every meaningful choice gets its rationale and the alternative I rejected.
  4. Check the source. If a claim cannot be traced, it does not ship. It gets flagged and checked.

Case A · Reference platform

A 507-term reference built like a product.

A searchable glossary with learning paths, guided walkthroughs, and a cross-platform comparison map. All of it renders from one data layer, with no framework and no build step.

Vanilla JS Static site Accessibility Content ops

Problem

New hires, sales engineers, and partner managers kept asking the same twenty questions. Existing docs described features; nobody described the mechanics: how money, contracts, and authorization move through a marketplace deal.

What I built

  • 507 terms with aliases, difficulty, category, audience, use cases, and 1,477 curated cross-links.
  • 21 learning paths (269 steps) sequenced by role and prerequisite, with per-step progress saved locally.
  • 37 guided walkthroughs (194 steps) with 486 “before you move on” checks. The operational layer the glossary alone could not provide.
  • 36 comparison rows across up to seven platforms, derived from the alias graph instead of a second hand-maintained table.
  • A What’s New feed: 1,761 entries from 13 monitored sources, ingested by a scheduled Node script.

Key decisions

No framework, no build. Vanilla ES modules and one CSS file. A content site with no state to manage did not need a bundler, and the constraint kept deployment to a push to main.
One data layer, eight pages. Terms, tags, paths, and walkthroughs live in plain JS modules. The comparison view is derived from alias syntax with a union-find pass, so adding a term can improve three pages at once.
Do not guess. Every term carries a source URL. If a claim had no authoritative source, it stayed out, even when a stakeholder asked for it twice.

Scope and limits

A static, solo-built prototype. There is no server, auth, analytics pipeline, or load testing. terms.js is ~795 KB and loads before first paint with no code-splitting. Impact was not measured; the evidence is qualitative. Some accessibility work is incomplete.

Case B · Publishing

A briefing that explains what changed and why it matters.

Five editions of a monthly briefing. Each picks two or three themes, compares how AWS, Microsoft, Google, and Snowflake approach them, and ends with what it means for a specific reader.

Editorial design Data-driven rendering Export & print Persona model

Problem

Marketplace rules change constantly: fee structures, co-sell eligibility, committed-spend rules. A weekly changelog was accurate and useless. It told people what happened without telling them whether to care.

What I built

  • A two-layer architecture: a scannable feed for triage and a long-form edition page for depth, sharing one data layer.
  • 43 topic objects across 5 editions (15 published), ~6,300 words of published analysis, every hyperscaler section carrying a source.
  • A persona model (Explorer / Tracker) that reorders the page by intent: learning or monitoring.
  • Export to self-contained HTML and print/PDF, because the real distribution channel was email and a forwarded file, not a website.
  • A glossary drawer embedded as a 480px iframe, so a reader can resolve a term without losing their place.

Key decisions

Topic-first, not platform-first. A section per hyperscaler would have been easier to write and worse to read. Organizing by theme forces the comparison and surfaces where the platforms diverge.
Every claim gets a source, inline. Not a bibliography at the bottom. The link sits under the claim it supports, because the reader’s next move is usually “show me.”
Reuse the glossary instead of rewriting it. Definitions live in one place. The briefing links into the reference instead of maintaining a second, drifting copy.

Scope and limits

A nine-day prototype built with sample data. The editions show the architecture and editorial model. They were not distributed to customers, and there are no open or click metrics. The real channel was email and a forwarded file, which is why export and print exist.

Case C · Curriculum

A seven-week onboarding curriculum, sourced before it was written.

A path from marketplace concepts to co-sell judgment. Thirteen modules and thirteen quizzes are drafted. All six content weeks are source-mapped, with gaps marked.

Instructional design Curriculum architecture Assessment Source mapping

Problem

New hires were expected to learn a contractual, regional, multi-platform product by shadowing. There was no reading order, no shared vocabulary, and no way to check whether someone understood a process before putting them in front of a customer. The source material existed in a knowledge base, product docs, call recordings, and three people’s notes, but it had never been sequenced.

What I built

  • 13 modules and 13 quizzes (146 questions), paired one-to-one by folder. The first four content weeks are drafted; weeks 5–6 are mapped but not yet written.
  • A 246-row source map covering weeks 1–6, with two columns per topic (a company source and an official platform source), and gaps marked explicitly.
  • ~17,000 words of module prose using one skeleton: why it matters, mental model, mechanics, worked example, and a check for understanding.
  • An answer key kept out of the quiz files: Week 1–2 keys are stored as SHA-256 hashes in a separate file, with the limitation stated in the file. That is obfuscation, not security.
  • 108 commits over four weeks, with a changelog and per-change diff backups for every module and quiz.

Key decisions

Source map before prose. The first artifact was a two-column source map, not a lesson. If a topic had no authoritative source, it was marked as a gap instead of filled with plausible prose.
Make the pairing rule structural. Every module folder has a matching quiz folder, and a module without a quiz fails review. No orphan modules or quizzes.
Keep answers out of the quiz files. Week 1–2 keys live as SHA-256 hashes in a separate file. The file says plainly that this is obfuscation, not security. The point is to keep casual viewers from reading answers in the quiz source.
Keep drafting and publishing separate. The repository is the drafting layer. Publishing to the internal LMS is an explicit, approved step. Nothing is live by default.

Scope and limits

Drafted and reviewed, not delivered to a live cohort. Weeks 5–6 and the capstone are mapped but not written. There is no completion, retention, or assessment-outcome data yet. This case covers the design, sourcing, and assessment structure.

Case D · Operations model

An AI content pipeline built around the review gate.

Seven agent specifications, three skill documents, and a two-gate review process. A repeatable way to produce research-heavy content without publishing hallucinations.

AI operations Process design Prompt engineering Editorial standards

Problem

Research-heavy content has two failure modes: it is slow, or it is confidently wrong. LLM drafting solved the first and made the second worse. A plausible sentence about a fee schedule looks the same as a verified one until someone checks.

What I built

  • Seven agent specs (554 lines), each with a typed output schema, confidence field, and explicit failure behavior.
  • Three skill documents: topic discovery (198 lines), UX outline (263), and a writing guide (777) with six content types, worked examples, and persona reframings.
  • A two-gate review model: the reasoning report is reviewed before the draft is opened, so a bad topic is rejected in one read.
  • An anti-approximation rule in the writing guide: if you cannot cite an exact number, you do not publish. You flag it [VERIFY].
  • Two documented pipeline runs producing 3 drafts and 10 topics, including a verification pass that caught a bad CAGR figure, a press release from the wrong year, and two wrong product versions.

Key decisions

Review the reasoning first. Reviewing the reasoning report first makes rejection cheap and turns the alternatives list into a swap mechanism. This was the most useful idea in the system.
Define every handoff. Every handoff has a JSON schema, a confidence level, and a defined failure mode. “Never output unchanged if the hash comparison was skipped” is the kind of rule that prevents silent corruption.
Write for the newcomer. When in doubt, the writing guide defaults to the 101 reader. Practitioners can skim an explanation; newcomers cannot fill a gap they do not know exists.

Scope and limits

This was a designed and piloted operating model, not an automated system. Agents ran as prompted sessions with a human in the loop. There was no orchestration code, scheduler, or evaluation harness. The value is in the contracts and the review design, and in knowing what was missing.

06

How the sites are built.

The reference and briefing sites are static: no framework, no bundler, and no server. That was a deliberate trade. Less abstraction to maintain, at the cost of hand-rolling the parts a framework would give you.

Data is the source of truth

Terms, paths, and walkthroughs are plain JS modules with a documented schema. Rendering derives from data: comparison groups, tag colors, and section ordering are computed, not duplicated. Term difficulty is assigned per term against a rubric.

Search degrades gracefully

Exact substring matching runs first. When it returns nothing, a subsequence scorer takes over, weighting term names three times higher than body text. So “cppp” still finds CPPO without a search index or a dependency.

Accessibility is a constraint

Keyboard-operable cards, focus trapping in modals, aria-live regions for filtered results, 44px touch targets, skip links, and a documented contrast floor. Some of it is imperfect (see Limits), but it was designed in, not bolted on.

Failure modes are explicit

The ingestion script aborts instead of overwriting the archive when a source parses to zero entries. That guard was added after a near-miss that would have deleted 1,607 records. Per-source merge semantics exist because “replace” was silently dropping posts.

app/search.js — subsequence scoring vanilla JS, no dependencies 
// Subsequence fuzzy match — returns a score > 0 if all query chars appear
// in order within text, rewarding consecutive runs. Returns 0 on no match.
function fuzzyScore(text, query) {
  if (!text) return 0;
  const t = text.toLowerCase();
  const q = query.toLowerCase();
  let ti = 0, qi = 0, score = 0, consecutive = 0;
  while (ti < t.length && qi < q.length) {
    if (t[ti] === q[qi]) {
      score += 1 + consecutive;   // reward adjacent hits
      consecutive++;
      qi++;
    } else {
      consecutive = 0;
    }
    ti++;
  }
  return qi === q.length ? score : 0;
}

08

Scope and limits.

These are the gaps I would fix first and the things a reviewer should not assume.

Limitations

  • Prototypes, not production systems. The reference, briefing, and curriculum projects are static and solo-built. There is no server, no auth, no analytics pipeline, and no load testing.
  • The curriculum is drafted, not delivered. Weeks 5–6 and the capstone are mapped but not written. There is no completion, retention, or assessment-outcome data yet.
  • No automated tests. Quality was enforced through schema rules, runtime guards, and human review.
  • The reference loads all of its data up front. terms.js is ~795 KB and whats-new.js is ~842 KB. Both load before first paint on their pages, with no code-splitting or prerender. It is optimized for authoring speed, not first-load performance.
  • I did not measure impact. There is no usage analytics, completion data, or before/after count of repeat questions. The evidence is qualitative: the reference was embedded in the enablement flow, requests came through the glossary, and defects were reported and fixed. If I rebuilt it, I would instrument zero-result searches, term-detail views, path completion, and repeat-question volume from day one.
  • The AI pipeline was piloted, not automated. Agents ran as prompted sessions. There was no orchestration code or evaluation harness, and no output reached publication without a human gate.
  • The briefing prototype still has rough edges. The persona control reorders instead of filtering (All and Tracker produce the same order), 28 drafted topic objects were left orphaned, and dark-mode print did not reset the colors. Those are prototype costs, not hidden defects.
  • Documentation drifted. A few counts and claims in the docs fell behind the code. That is a real maintenance cost of writing ~15,000 lines of process documentation.
  • Some accessibility work is incomplete. No reduced-motion support in one product, incomplete focus management in two modals, and contrast claims that were partly aspirational.
  • The evergreen guide was structured but never populated. 23 sections defined with verification semantics; zero sections verified before the project ended.

I’m looking for work in knowledge systems, enablement, or front-end development.

I like problems where the documentation is the product: complex domains, technical audiences, and a real need for the answer to be correct.
